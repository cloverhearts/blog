When conversation became quiet, I often felt responsible for fixing it. I searched for something funny and tried to keep the mood alive. The more I tried to entertain the other person, the less carefully I heard what they were saying.

One day I changed the approach. Instead of trying to make someone laugh, I talked about something I could enjoy and let myself laugh naturally at their story.

## A comfortable person rather than an entertaining one

When laughter becomes a goal, conversation turns into a small performance. A strong reaction seems more important than whether people understood one another. Silence feels like failure, and preparing the next line makes the current story easy to miss.

Relaxing reduced the urge to fill every pause. There was more room to ask questions instead of evaluating or covering the other person’s story with a better one.

People shared a little more. I had not suddenly become a better speaker. The purpose had changed from “I must control the mood” to “we can make it comfortable to talk.”

## Laughter can be permission, not only a result

I had treated laughter as a reward for a funny line. It can also signal that no one needs to perform perfectly. When one person releases tension, the other may feel less pressure to present a perfectly organized story.

Laughing is not appropriate in every situation. Serious stories should not be made light. The point was not to force a cheerful expression but to release some of the desire to control the response.

## Change the strategy for connection

Good conversation does not always need an impressive topic. Listening without interrupting, asking about what is unclear, and not exaggerating reactions can remain longer.

When I tried to entertain, the central question was how I appeared. When I relaxed first, the central question became what kind of time we were sharing. Trying to be comfortable to talk with suited me better than trying to be entertaining—and laughter appeared more often after the pressure was removed.
