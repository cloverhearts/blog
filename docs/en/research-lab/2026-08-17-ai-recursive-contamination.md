AI makes it easy to process writing. A long document can be summarized, the summary can be classified, and those notes can become the input for another article. The problem is that each cycle makes it harder to tell what came from the source and what was added through interpretation.

I use the phrase “recursive AI contamination” to describe this pattern. It is not presented here as a formal academic term. It simply names a cycle in which AI output becomes the next AI system’s input, and the new output is fed into another round.

## Summarization does more than remove information

A summary may look like a shorter copy of the original, but it also contains a judgment about importance. The moment an AI keeps one sentence and drops another, interpretation has already entered the result.

Context removed from the first summary no longer exists for the second one. The next AI cannot know what disappeared. It can only turn the remaining text into a smoother conclusion. The prose may become easier to read while moving further away from the original.

This is difficult to notice because the error may still sound natural. A fabricated number is easy to question. A missing condition or a cautious statement rewritten as certainty can pass without attracting attention.

## Keep three kinds of documents separate

One way to reduce this risk is to avoid storing every note at the same level.

| Type | What it contains | What to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Source | Direct records, collected documents, quotations | Are the source and date preserved? |
| Human interpretation | Reasons, judgments, and opinions | Are facts and opinions distinguished? |
| AI output | Summaries, classifications, drafts, inferences | Is the input that produced it recorded? |

The goal is not to discard AI output. It is to use it actively without treating it as a source. An AI summary should link back to its input, and important claims should show whether a person has verified them.

## Human verification is not a final spelling check

In an AI-assisted knowledge base, the human role is more than reading the final result once. People must decide what counts as a source, which interpretations should be retained, and when a process must return to the original material.

Three questions make a useful review checklist:

1. Does this statement actually appear in the source?
2. Are the source’s conditions and exceptions still present?
3. Was this conclusion produced only from other AI outputs?

AI can extend memory, but it cannot guarantee the provenance of that memory. As a knowledge base grows, the path back to the source and a visible record of human verification matter more than adding another layer of automation.
